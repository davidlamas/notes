# Ways of Knowing in HCI: conceptual taxonomy

## Purpose

This taxonomy is a teaching device for doctoral students, not a claim that HCI can be partitioned into mutually exclusive schools. It organizes research by the commitments that make a knowledge claim warranted: what is assumed to exist, how a researcher can know it, what counts as evidence, and what standards make a claim credible.

The source textbook grounds the map's account of HCI as an interdisciplinary field that combines empirical and artifact contributions, works across micro- and macro-HCI, uses qualitative and quantitative evidence, faces recurring trade-offs, and gains confidence through complementary studies and triangulation. Because the textbook is primarily organized by method, the epistemological families below are synthesized with foundational HCI scholarship.

## The six families

### 1. Empirical-analytic

- **Ontology:** phenomena can be operationalized as stable enough entities, variables, behaviours, or regularities to compare.
- **Epistemology:** systematic observation, measurement, modelling, and controlled comparison can produce increasingly reliable explanations or predictions.
- **Knowledge/evidence:** effects, estimates, models, performance measures, error patterns, reproducible observations, and falsifiable propositions.
- **Characteristic questions:** What changes what? By how much? Under which conditions? Can performance or behaviour be predicted?
- **Typical methods:** experiments, quasi-experiments, surveys, telemetry, benchmarking, physiological measurement, statistical and computational modelling.
- **Claims/contributions:** causal or correlational explanation, prediction, validated guideline, model, metric, or generalizable regularity.
- **Quality:** construct validity, internal/external validity, reliability, statistical conclusion validity, transparency, replication or robustness.
- **Risks:** mistaking an operational measure for the phenomenon; stripping away context; treating statistical significance as practical importance; over-generalizing from narrow populations.

### 2. Interpretive-situated

- **Ontology:** meanings and practices are situated, socially produced, historically formed, and locally accomplished.
- **Epistemology:** knowledge develops through interpretation of action in context; the researcher is part of the interpretive situation rather than a neutral recording instrument.
- **Knowledge/evidence:** thick description, participant accounts, observed practices, interactional detail, field records, and theoretically informed interpretations.
- **Characteristic questions:** How is this technology understood and enacted here? How do people make a practice work? What meanings organize experience?
- **Typical methods:** ethnography, contextual inquiry, interviews, participant observation, discourse or interaction analysis, interpretive case study, grounded analysis.
- **Claims/contributions:** situated explanation, conceptual reframing, account of practice, sensitising concept, or theory grounded in a setting.
- **Quality:** credibility, reflexivity, contextual depth, analytic coherence, negative-case attention, resonance, and transferability rather than statistical representativeness.
- **Risks:** treating interpretation as mere anecdote; hiding the researcher's position; translating rich accounts prematurely into decontextualized requirements.

### 3. Constructive-designerly

- **Ontology:** possible worlds and preferred futures can be made materially available for inquiry; artifacts participate in articulating a problem.
- **Epistemology:** designing and making can generate knowledge that analysis alone cannot; reflection on a disciplined design process is part of the warrant.
- **Knowledge/evidence:** annotated artifacts, design process records, deployments, critiques, portfolios, experiential encounters, and reflections across iterations.
- **Characteristic questions:** What becomes thinkable or experienceable if we make this? How can an artifact reframe a design space? What knowledge is embodied in the design?
- **Typical methods:** research through design, constructive design research, speculative design, probes, prototyping, design fiction, annotated portfolios.
- **Claims/contributions:** artifact, design exemplar, strong concept, design space, generative framework, or material speculation.
- **Quality:** rigour in process, transparency, relevance, novelty, generativity, conceptual coherence, and the artifact's capacity to open inquiry.
- **Risks:** presenting novelty as knowledge without articulation; hiding failed iterations; evaluating a research artifact only as a product.

### 4. Critical-transformative

- **Ontology:** technologies and knowledge practices are value-laden and embedded in relations of power; categories and infrastructures can reproduce inequity.
- **Epistemology:** critique can expose what dominant framings make invisible, while reflexive and value-explicit design can help transform conditions.
- **Knowledge/evidence:** exclusions, breakdowns, counter-readings, historical traces, lived consequences, standpoint accounts, and value conflicts.
- **Characteristic questions:** Who benefits, who is burdened, and whose knowledge is missing? What assumptions have been normalized? How might conditions be otherwise?
- **Typical methods:** critical discourse analysis, feminist and postcolonial analysis, critical technical practice, value-sensitive inquiry, adversarial or speculative design, participatory critique.
- **Claims/contributions:** critique, counter-narrative, value framework, disclosure of harm, alternative framing, or emancipatory design direction.
- **Quality:** positional clarity, accountability, intersectional attention, evidential grounding, reflexivity, and consequential relevance.
- **Risks:** critique without accountable engagement; universalizing a standpoint; treating values as a checklist; speaking for affected people.

### 5. Participatory-democratic

- **Ontology:** people affected by technology hold indispensable situated expertise; design situations include contested interests and possibilities for collective action.
- **Epistemology:** knowledge is co-produced through participation, mutual learning, and action; who may shape the inquiry is an epistemic and political question.
- **Knowledge/evidence:** co-created artifacts, workshop traces, participant decisions, changes in practice, negotiated accounts, and outcomes meaningful to collaborators.
- **Characteristic questions:** Who should shape this technology and on what terms? What can collaborators learn and change together? How can participation alter power?
- **Typical methods:** participatory design, cooperative inquiry, co-design, action research, community-based research, future workshops.
- **Claims/contributions:** shared understanding, capacity, negotiated design, local change, democratic process, or transferable participatory method.
- **Quality:** genuine influence, reciprocity, accessibility, transparency of decision rights, sustained accountability, and benefit to collaborators.
- **Risks:** token participation; confusing attendance with power; extractive workshops; concealing conflicts behind consensus language.

### 6. Relational-entangled

- **Ontology:** humans, technologies, institutions, data, and material arrangements do not precede their relations as fully separate entities; agency and responsibility are distributed but not erased.
- **Epistemology:** knowing is situated within sociomaterial relations and is performative: inquiry helps enact the phenomenon it studies.
- **Knowledge/evidence:** relations, enactments, material configurations, breakdowns, traces, more-than-human effects, and accountable interventions.
- **Characteristic questions:** How do people and technologies become with one another? What realities does this apparatus enact? Where do accountability and response-ability emerge?
- **Typical methods:** thing-centred and posthuman design, material speculation, diffractive analysis, actor-network-informed tracing, long-term deployments, relational ethnography.
- **Claims/contributions:** relational account, reconfigured unit of analysis, accountable intervention, material speculation, or alternative human-technology relation.
- **Quality:** relational specificity, attentiveness to materiality, traceable accountabilities, reflexivity about the apparatus, and care in representing nonhuman agency.
- **Risks:** flattening unequal power into symmetrical networks; abstract language without empirical purchase; using entanglement to diffuse responsibility.

## Bridges rather than boxes

- **Pragmatism** asks what combination of inquiry helps address a consequential problem; it does not make methods epistemically neutral.
- **Mixed methods** combines forms of evidence, but integration requires explaining how their assumptions and warrants relate.
- **Triangulation** can seek convergence, complementarity, contradiction, or expansion; disagreement between methods can be a finding.
- **Technical/formal work** often aligns with empirical-analytic commitments but can also serve constructive, participatory, critical, or relational projects.
- **Ethics** is not a separate final step. It shapes who defines the problem, what is measured, which harms count, and how claims travel.

## Cross-family relations represented in the map

1. Empirical-analytic ↔ interpretive-situated: translation between controlled regularity and contextual meaning; complementary but with different standards of generalization.
2. Interpretive-situated ↔ participatory-democratic: shared attention to situated knowledge; participation adds co-determination and action.
3. Constructive-designerly ↔ empirical-analytic: artifacts can instantiate hypotheses and then be evaluated, but product performance is not the only warrant for designerly knowledge.
4. Constructive-designerly ↔ critical-transformative: speculative and adversarial artifacts can materialize critique and alternatives.
5. Critical-transformative ↔ participatory-democratic: common concern with voice and power; tension remains over who frames the critique and controls outcomes.
6. Relational-entangled ↔ interpretive-situated: both reject context-free accounts; relational work further unsettles a fixed human/technology boundary.
7. Relational-entangled ↔ critical-transformative: both trace how realities are produced; critical work keeps asymmetry, justice, and responsibility explicit.
8. Participatory-democratic ↔ constructive-designerly: making together supports mutual learning, while participation demands more than eliciting design inspiration.

## Historical overlays

Historical schemes appear as optional lenses, not as the map's hierarchy.

- **Bødker's waves:** first-wave human factors and individual performance; second-wave situated work, cooperation, and human actors; third-wave everyday life, experience, multiplicity, and participation. The waves coexist.
- **Harrison, Tatar, and Sengers' paradigms:** human factors; classical cognitivism/information processing; phenomenologically situated HCI. This is related to, but not identical with, Bødker's waves.
- **Rogers on theory:** theory can explain, predict, describe, sensitize, critique, generate design, and frame new phenomena; no single role is the universal destination of HCI theory.
- **Kuutti and Bannon's practice turn:** shifts attention from bounded interaction episodes toward historically and materially constituted practices.
- **Frauenberger's Entanglement HCI:** a proposed next wave grounded in relational ontology, performativity, materiality, and accountability. It is a proposal, not an established fourth stage that supersedes prior work.

## Representative study selection rule

Examples are selected because the paper makes its warrant visible, not merely because it uses a recognizable method. The collection balances CHI, CSCW, DIS, UbiComp, IUI, and related SIGCHI venues, favors DOI or ACM links, and includes both canonical methodological statements and studies that enact a tradition. Each example is accompanied by a short explanation of why it belongs near a family while acknowledging overlaps.
