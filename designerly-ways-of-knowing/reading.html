The argument

S01

01. What can we learn by trying to change a situation?

A proposed arrangement can make a relationship visible, expose a difficulty or change what seems worth pursuing. Designing can contribute to understanding through this traffic between a question, a move and its consequences. The inquiry begins with that relation.

Keep in view

This is a synthesis across accounts of designing, not a claim that every act of making produces research knowledge.

Grounds: C01, C03, C07.

Concepts: Designerly ways of knowing, Inquiry and intervention, Material engagement

Next in the argument: Any proposed change already contains a judgement about what would be appropriate.

S02

02. Appropriate for whom, and under what conditions?

Design concerns arrangements in a human-made world. Cross emphasised constructive inquiry and judgements of appropriateness. Such judgements join purposes, people and circumstances: an arrangement can serve one group while burdening another. Technical ingenuity does not settle which ends should prevail.

Keep in view

Cross's comparison of intellectual cultures is a heuristic. Science and the humanities also construct, interpret and imagine.

Grounds: C01, C08, C22.

Concepts: Designerly ways of knowing, Ill-defined and wicked problems, Epistemic authority and power

Next in the argument: Before proposing an arrangement, someone has selected what counts as the problem.

S03

03. A frame selects what matters.

A frame names relevant features, identifies a difficulty and makes some actions seem worth trying. It also leaves things outside the question. Reframing changes those selections. It can follow a surprise, an unsuccessful move or a perspective the initial account overlooked.

Keep in view

A persuasive reframe is a proposal about the situation, not discovery of its uniquely correct hidden problem.

Grounds: C04, C08.

Concepts: Framing and reframing, Epistemic authority and power

Next in the argument: Possible responses can then change the problem that a frame makes visible.

S04

04. Problems and possible solutions can develop together.

A possible response can reveal a new constraint or opportunity. That changes the understanding of the problem, which redirects the next response. Dorst and Cross examined this co-evolution in a study of nine experienced industrial designers working on a bounded task.

Keep in view

The study supports an interpreted relationship in that setting. It does not establish a mandatory sequence or prove superiority over every alternative.

Grounds: C05.

Concepts: Framing and reframing, Problem-solution co-evolution

Next in the argument: The reasoning that proposes a possible arrangement remains conjectural.

S05

05. A constructive proposal connects means with something valued.

In Dorst's account, designing may require proposing both an arrangement and a working principle through which it could achieve a valued outcome. This is constructive abduction. Deductions can explore expected consequences, and observations can challenge the proposal. These forms of reasoning work together.

Keep in view

A plausible connection is not proof that the value is shared or the effect will occur.

Grounds: C02.

Concepts: Constructive abduction, Deduction, Induction

Next in the argument: Reasoning also occurs through practical activity that may precede a full verbal account.

S06

06. Practical knowing need not begin as a verbal rule.

Schön describes knowing in action: competent judging and adjusting that need not be preceded by an explicit account of how to act. People may later describe aspects of that knowing. The description is a reconstruction, not transparent access to the understanding that directed every move.

Keep in view

Being unable to explain an action does not make it more reliable, skilled or true.

Grounds: C03.

Concepts: Knowing in action, Tacit knowing

Next in the argument: Unexpected consequences can make practical understanding available for reconsideration.

S07

07. Reflection can change the next move.

A situation can surprise a practitioner while action remains possible. Reflection in action questions a judgement, makes another move and attends to what follows. Reflection on action revisits an activity afterward. The distinction concerns the relation to action, not simply fast versus slow thought.

Keep in view

A successful move can affirm a direction without confirming the causal explanation attached to it.

Grounds: C03, C07.

Concepts: Reflection in action, Reflection on action, Framing and reframing

Next in the argument: Representations give this conversation a material form that can be inspected again.

S08

08. Representations can help develop thought.

A sketch, model or enactment can make a relationship perceptible before it has been fully formulated in words. Making does more than display a finished idea. In Suwa and Tversky's small sketch study, drawings and retrospective accounts together supported analysis of what participants noticed.

Keep in view

The evidence does not show that images alone reveal thought or that sketching is always superior to other forms of inquiry.

Grounds: C06, C13.

Concepts: Material engagement, Externalisation, Embodied knowing

Next in the argument: What can be learned depends on which consequences the representation makes available.

S09

09. Consequences have a scope.

A relation noticed in a drawing, a difficulty encountered in a mock-up and a change observed in an operating service are different kinds of evidence. Simplification makes exploration manageable. It can also omit costs, institutions, materials and people that matter in practice.

Keep in view

Success within a representation or encounter does not by itself establish success in the implemented world.

Grounds: C07, C11.

Concepts: Material engagement, Prototypes, Situated and actionable knowledge

Next in the argument: The same care is needed when describing what a person knows or can express.

S10

10. Expression is partial and constructive.

The generative literature distinguishes explicit, observable, tacit and latent forms of knowing. These terms are not a settled set of hidden objects. Tacit knowing concerns limits of articulation; latent language may concern needs or future possibilities. A later expression can be developed through the inquiry itself.

Keep in view

Making does not establish complete access to a person's tacit knowing or recover a future preference intact.

Grounds: C14, C24, C28.

Concepts: Explicit accounts, Observable activity, Tacit knowing, Latent possibilities, Externalisation

Next in the argument: Different expressive modes create complementary opportunities and different interpretive demands.

S11

11. Saying, doing, making, enacting and imagining can inform one another.

Saying can explain reasons and meanings. Doing shows activity under particular conditions. Making gives a possibility form. Enacting brings bodies, timing and sequence into view. Imagining explores what is absent or not yet experienced. These modes overlap within encounters and often require each other.

Keep in view

No mode occupies a higher level of truth. Observation and material form also require interpretation.

Grounds: C12, C13, C28.

Concepts: Explicit accounts, Observable activity, Embodied knowing, Externalisation, Imagination and imaginaries

Next in the argument: Generative design research deliberately arranges such encounters.

S12

12. Generative research arranges an inquiry with people.

In the Sanders, Stappers and Sleeswijk Visser lineage, generative design research invites people to explore experience and possible futures to inform design. It includes preparation, encounters, documentation, interpretation and communication. Co-creation names shared creativity; co-design places collective creativity within designing.

Keep in view

These terms overlap but are not interchangeable. A workshop or toolkit alone does not establish a research contribution.

Grounds: C09, C10, C11, C33.

Concepts: Generative design research, Contextmapping, Participation, co-creation and co-design, Designed conditions

Next in the argument: The encounter often begins before people meet.

S13

13. Preparation supports attention and also directs it.

Sensitising can give people time to notice, remember and develop accounts before a collective session. Diaries, booklets and short tasks supply prompts and recall cues. They also focus attention and require effort. What later becomes expressible belongs to that history of preparation.

Keep in view

This is a methodological rationale, not a law that preparation necessarily produces deeper or more accurate knowledge.

Next in the argument: Materials within the encounter continue to shape what can be expressed.

S14

14. Tools enable expression partly by constraining it.

A finite set of images, words or forms can make it easier to begin and combine ideas. Ambiguity may invite association; incompleteness may invite alteration. Content, abstraction and cultural references still shape the available expression. Too much choice or an inaccessible task can make participation harder.

Keep in view

An open invitation is designed. Its usefulness depends on people, purpose and setting.

Grounds: C16, C21, C23.

Concepts: Ambiguity, Openness and incompleteness, Metaphor and association, Tools and generative toolkits, Facilitation

Next in the argument: Similar materials can serve different research purposes.

S15

15. A material's purpose changes its evidence contract.

A probe can preserve uncertainty for inspiration. Contextmapping links making and telling for interpretation. A prototype can explore a developing possibility. A provotype can question assumptions in current practice. The same object may serve different purposes in different encounters.

Keep in view

Gaver's probe argument and contextmapping's analytical commitments should not be merged into a single method for extracting hidden knowledge.

Grounds: C17, C18, C11.

Concepts: Contextmapping, Cultural probes, Prototypes, Provotypes

Next in the argument: Individual material then changes when other people encounter and work with it.

S16

16. Collective work transforms individual contributions.

An account may be explained, extended, contested, selected or ignored in group work. Shared material can sustain coordination across different views. A completed group artefact records a collective product, but does not reveal every contribution, silence or negotiation that made it possible.

Keep in view

Coordination and consensus are different. Shared authorship and unanimous agreement require their own evidence.

Grounds: C19, C20.

Next in the argument: Who can contribute, and whose interpretation prevails, depends on the arrangement.

S17

17. Participation distributes opportunities and authority unevenly.

Recruitment, language, time, facilitation and materials affect what can be contributed. Researchers may still control questions, categories and circulation. Institutions retain decision powers outside the room. Consent to participate, permission to record and permission for public media use also differ.

Keep in view

Being heard respectfully is consequential, but it does not establish equal control over the inquiry or its consequences.

S18

18. An artefact needs an account and a context.

For an analytical use of generative material, retain the prompt, circumstances, maker's account and relevant interaction. A photograph alone cannot explain what an arrangement meant. Provenance makes the material's origin recoverable; interpretive traceability connects it to the claim later made.

Keep in view

Traceability need not expose private records. It also does not make an interpretation neutral or correct.

Grounds: C23, C25, C39, C40.

S19

19. Interpretation is made through accountable judgement.

Researchers select material, segment it, compare instances and construct categories or themes. Those operations change what the material can mean and do. An observation, interpretation, implication and design idea should remain distinguishable so that a reader can examine the move between them.

Keep in view

Constructed meaning need not be arbitrary. The question is whether the reading is defensible and open to challenge.

Grounds: C24, C25, C26.

Concepts: Artefacts, traces, data and evidence, Analysis and interpretation, Provenance and traceability

S20

20. Disagreement can change the account.

Comparing people, methods and records can challenge an interpretation as well as corroborate it. Preserve material that does not fit, distinguish alternative readings and return to the source. Agreement among related records may reflect shared prompts or participants rather than independent confirmation.

Keep in view

Triangulation does not turn a claim into objective truth automatically. Contradictions may remain unresolved.

Grounds: C26, C27, C38.

Concepts: Analysis and interpretation, Triangulation and counterinstances

S21

21. A contribution needs a warrant suited to its purpose.

A situated account, reframing, possibility, prototype and claim of effectiveness ask different things of evidence. Research through design contains disagreement about how contributions should accumulate: Zimmerman's assessment lenses and Gaver's provisional, contingent accounts are not one settled standard.

Keep in view

Usefulness to a design process does not establish prevalence, causal benefit or institutional impact.

22. Democracy Labs designed conditions for democratic inquiry.

Within INCITE-DEM, six European Lab settings invited people to explore participation procedures and possible futures. Citizens worked with facilitators and country teams in a consortium-designed inquiry. Later research, institutional fora and public products transformed the material for different purposes and audiences.

Keep in view

The Labs did not themselves enact the institutions imagined. The five levels shown here are an analytical organisation of the case.

26. A group identifier supports continuity, not a causal story.

Barcelona group 4 reported an account of tenants excluded from an apartment-solar decision. The same published group later produced a Self-managed Food System. Researchers classified that proposal in the cross-country analysis. The group link is documented; the intervening reasoning is not fully available.

Keep in view

The earlier account cannot be said to have caused the later proposal. The researchers' classification is not automatically the participants' own label.

S27

27. Institutional circulation changes the proposal.

Researchers selected 20 of 31 innovations and merged them into six models for institutional fora. Democracy Allowance proposed monthly paid time with unmonitored use. Responses differed over frequency, accreditation, cost and equity. A later roadmap recommended a revised allowance and future pilots.

Keep in view

The full proposal-to-model and comment-to-revision histories are unavailable. Reception and recommendation do not establish adoption or effects.

S29

29. What can this inquiry support?

Designing can make relationships, judgements and possibilities available for understanding. Generative research arranges encounters in which such contributions can develop. Its claims remain answerable to the conditions, material, interpretations and transformations that produced them. A useful possibility can be situated, contestable and unfinished.

Keep in view

Neither making, participation nor publication establishes truth, equality or impact by itself.

Democracy Labs

Democracy Labs was a generative inquiry within INCITE-DEM into how people might participate in decisions and shape democratic institutions for sustainable futures. Six European Lab settings brought citizens together with facilitators, country teams and some institutional participants.

Grounds: DL01, DL02, DL70.

The inquiry began with preparation and continued through shared work, documentation, analysis and later public or institutional products. The 2024 Labs, the later institutional fora and the later game are different encounters with different participants and purposes.

Grounds: DL02, DL48, DL52, DL54.

You can follow selected accounts, expressions, group proposals, classifications and institutional responses. No inspected record joins one participant continuously from preparation to a political effect. Each journey shows what connects and where the evidence stops.

Grounds: DL31, DL51, DL58.

Keep in view

These five levels organise the evidence. They are not a five-step method or a ranking of certainty.

L1

Participant encounter

What became possible to articulate?

Prompted accounts, expressions and retrospective testimony.

Keep in view

An expression is situated; anonymous instruments do not create a biography.

L2

Facilitated Lab

How did contributions become shared work?

Canvases, timelines, wheels, stories and presentations.

Keep in view

A shared output does not expose every contribution or establish consensus.

L3

Country implementation

Who could participate, and under which conditions?

Recruitment, adaptation, language, venues, teams and local interpretation.

Keep in view

A site does not represent a country or explain differences by itself.

L4

Cross-country programme

What was retained and abstracted across sites?

Common protocol, translated material, categories, publications and later products.

Keep in view

Shared structure does not establish identical exposure, meaning or independent replication.

L5

Institutional and public horizon

Who could act on what travelled?

Rewritten models, institutional responses, public fictions and recommendations.

Keep in view

Reception and recommendation are not adoption or observed political effect.
